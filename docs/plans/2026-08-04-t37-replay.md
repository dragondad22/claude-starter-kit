# T37 replay — routing ShelterSync's decision corpus through the shipped artifacts

**Date:** 2026-08-04 · **Issue:** #215 (epic #205 exit criterion) · **Corpus:** ShelterSync —
90 decision-log entries, 54 ADRs

Epic #205 shipped a register, a routing rule, a proposal/register split and an assessment
gate. None of it had been tried on a project that did not already know the answer. This
replays a real corpus against the shipped artifacts and reports what happened.

## Method, and its limits

Each of the 90 decision-log entries was routed using only the shipped rule — the register's
*"would this still be true if this product were rebuilt on a different stack?"* test and its
outbound table — recording the destination(s), whether the rule decided on its own, and
whether the entry was a pointer to an ADR that already existed.

Two limits, stated up front:

- **Not blind.** Roughly 40 of these entries were classified earlier in the session that
  designed #206. This is a re-run by the designer, not an independent trial. Where a result
  flatters the design, discount it accordingly.
- **One corpus, one shape.** A decision log is not a spec and not a UAT doc. Two of the six
  questions #215 asked turn out not to be answerable from it at all — see *Not tested*.

## Findings

### 1. The missing step is decomposition, not routing — worth changing

The rule decided on its own only **23%** of the time, which looks damning until you read what
the other 58% actually were. Almost none were "the rule gave no answer." They were **entries
containing more than one statement**: a domain rule *and* a schema change, a vocabulary *and*
a permission, a rule *and* a scope call.

**59% of entries produced rows in two or more homes.** Once an entry is decomposed into
separate statements, each statement routes cleanly. The rule is not ambiguous — it is
answering a question nobody has framed yet.

The shipped register assumes you arrive holding one statement. Real decisions arrive as
compound documents. Nothing in the routing rule says *split the decision into statements
first, then route each one* — and that is the step doing the actual work.

### 2. The rebuild test cannot decide whether something is register material at all

Nine entries (10%) pass the rebuild test — they would absolutely still be true on another
stack — and belong nowhere near the register: a scope call (D1, D33), a term (D2, D82), a
working convention (D36, D69, D71), a licensing obligation (D72, D79).

The register presents the rebuild test as *the* routing rule, with "what belongs somewhere
else" as a supporting table underneath. That ordering is backwards for a tenth of real
traffic. The outbound table is the **first** question — *is this register material?* — and the
rebuild test is the **second** — *register or ADR?*

### 3. ADR is the largest single destination — the catchall diagnosis, quantified

**51 rows land in ADRs, more than the 50 that become business rules.** A document called a
decision log whose largest single output is architecture decisions is exactly what T37.2
diagnosed, now measured rather than asserted.

**Eight entries (9%) exist only to point at an ADR** — "Recorded in full as ADR-024" (D40),
"Recorded as ADR-025" (D41), ADR-028 (D46), ADR-031 (D49), ADR-047 (D78), ADR-048 (D80),
ADR-049/050 (D81), ADR-023 (D39). These do not move under the new model. They **vanish**,
because the ADR they cite already exists.

### 4. Four of the six register sections are essentially untested

`BR-` got 50 rows. `INV-` got **one**, `NFR-` **two**, `AC-` **zero**.

That is not evidence the sections are wrong — invariants, NFRs and acceptance criteria live
in specs, standards and UAT docs, which this corpus is not. But it means the replay validates
the **business-rule** section and says almost nothing about the other four. Any confidence in
`INV-`/`NFR-`/`UX-`/`AC-` still rests on design argument, not evidence.

### 5. Size: comfortably inside the estimate, but measuring a floor

67 rows against ~199 lines of register scaffolding is **≈266 lines** — well under the ~600
this was sized against in #206, and nowhere near needing a split. But this is a **floor**: a
real register also carries the acceptance criteria and invariants that come from specs, which
this corpus cannot supply. The one-file decision is not under pressure; it is also not yet
tested at full volume.

### 6. Story form did not get contorted — on a small sample

Only 8 entries became `US-` rows, and **none had to be forced**. The things with no natural
persona — RPO/RTO targets (D86), privileged AWS access (D87), quality-gate placement (D36),
the license (D72) — routed to `NFR-`, standards and the compliance register instead of being
bent into "as a user, I want…". The bet from #207 holds. On eight rows.

### 7. Two smaller observations

- **D14 and D35 are the same decision, recorded twice** — "require org selection before
  password validation", entries 21 apart, neither referencing the other. A within-corpus
  duplicate the log never noticed. Registers make this visible by construction; a chronological
  log does not.
- **D70 is not a decision.** "Kit 0.8.0 conform pass — decision log renamed, runbooks
  consolidated, domain Q&A moved" is a *record of work performed*. Nothing in the routing
  table covers "we did a thing", because nothing should — it belongs to a closed issue. Worth
  knowing that a decision log accumulates activity records, so a migration will meet them.

## Not tested

Two of #215's six questions cannot be answered by this corpus, and saying so is the result:

- **Where the durable / change-specific acceptance-criteria line falls.** The corpus contains
  **zero** acceptance criteria. Testing this needs specs plus UAT docs.
- **Whether `AC-` rows actually get filed mid-run.** This is a claim about behaviour during a
  verification run, not a property of any document. Nothing static can show it. It remains the
  weakest link in the epic — it asks someone to stop mid-verification and edit a different
  file — and it is now the **only** part with no evidence at all.

#215 was written assuming this corpus would speak to both. It does not. A second replay
against a spec-and-UAT corpus would be needed, and is worth doing before trusting the
acceptance-criteria half of #207.

## Re-run, after the fix (#222)

The routing rule was revised — decomposition named as the first move, the two questions put
in the order they resolve — and the corpus routed again. The unit of routing is now the
**statement**, which is the point of the change.

| | Pass 1 (as shipped) | Pass 2 (revised rule) |
|---|---|---|
| Unit | 90 entries | **149 statements** (1.66 per entry) |
| Decided by a shipped question | 23% | **96%** |
| Needed judgment | 58% | **4%** (6 statements) |

Destinations did not move — 67 register rows, 51 ADRs, same as pass 1. Nothing was
reclassified; what changed is that the rule now answers.

**The honest caveat:** decomposition is still work, and naming a step does not make it
mechanical. What the change removes is the *fight* — pass 1's judgment calls were mostly
"which home does this entry really belong to", a question with no answer because the entry
made several statements. Routing each statement has an answer 96% of the time.

### The six residuals were more interesting than the 96%

Re-adjudicating them against what the file actually says:

- **Four needed no file change at all.** D16 (device trust) is settled by the existing
  *"an invariant with no nameable evidence is a business rule"* tie-break. D58 (no
  proprietary clones) is settled by the outbound table's wording — compliance means
  imposed *from outside*, and this is self-imposed. D32 splits cleanly once decomposed.
  **I had failed to apply rules the register already contained.**

  That is a finding about the document, not about me: **the tie-breaks are real but
  unfindable.** They live inside section bodies, so at the moment you are choosing a
  section you do not know they exist. Fixed by one line where the choice is made.

- **Two were genuine gaps**, both now closed:
  - **`BR-` vs `NFR-` had no tie-break.** D26's storage quotas (1 GB/file, 100 GB/org)
    carry numbers, and `NFR-` said "quality targets, each with a number". A number does not
    make something an NFR — an enforced quota is a rule the domain imposes, not a standard
    the product is measured against. Stated now.
  - **Seed, demo and fixture content had no home.** D66 ("the demo org is a fully-populated
    believable small shelter") fits no row. It is a property of the build, not the product —
    the rules do not change because a demo org exists. Added to the outbound table.

- **One was not a routing problem.** D70 is an activity record ("conform pass done"), which
  belongs to a closed issue. Correctly uncovered by the table; see finding 7.

With those three edits, all 149 statements route without judgment. **That number should be
distrusted**: it is the designer scoring their own work on a corpus seen twice. The useful
result is not the 100% — it is that the second pass found two real gaps and one structural
problem the first pass had hidden inside a 58% aggregate.

## What follows

- **#222 implemented** for findings 1 and 2 — decomposition named as the first move, and the
  outbound question asked before the rebuild test.
- **Three further edits from the re-run**: tie-breaks made findable at the point of choice, a
  `BR-`/`NFR-` tie-break, and a home for seed/demo/fixture content.
- Findings 3–7 are recorded here and need no change: 3 confirms the design, 4 and 5 are
  scope limits on the evidence, 6 and 7 are observations.
- The epic's exit condition is met — a rule written in a feature spec has one permanent home
  reachable by ID, and the step that puts it there is gated. The routing rule needs the
  sharpening above to be usable by someone meeting it for the first time.
