# Changelog

All notable changes to the Claude Starter Kit are documented here.

The format is based on [Keep a Changelog](https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.1.0/),
and this project adheres to Semantic Versioning.

## [Unreleased]

### Added

- **`ai/STANDARDS/RELEASE_STANDARD.md` — a release is a promise to a named audience** (#225, epic #224, ADR-001). The kit defined MINOR and PATCH mechanically and **had no mechanism that could ever produce a MAJOR**: `release.sh` reads which `[Unreleased]` sections have entries, and the "After 1.0" section was dormant, instructing a human to *"flip this section to active when the team cuts 1.0"* — a setup block with no workflow owner, the exact failure class epic #172 exists to stop. Underneath it sat a mismatch found by working a real 75-issue backlog rather than theorising: **SemVer versions a contract, but most projects ship a product.** SemVer answers *"will upgrading break my integration?"* for one audience and answers it well; it cannot answer *"is it ready?"*, which is the question a stakeholder actually asks — so a project either drifts on `0.x` indefinitely (advertising itself as unstable long after it stopped being) or bumps MAJOR on feel. The new standard names the thing the number counts: **a promise to a named audience, stated in one sentence**, which names every party it must satisfy *including the business* — a promise phrased only in the user's terms describes software, not a product ("if there is no way to sign up, there is no product; there is a website and an application"). **A product has several releases in sequence** — limited availability before general availability — and the derivation found two of them colliding in one undefined "1.0", which is why that question had gone unanswered for months: it was two questions wearing one label. **"Pre-release" is named as the framing to resist**, because a limited-availability release sheds *commercial* scope and not *readiness* scope, and the pilot depends on it in production. **Two archetypes ship, and a project may be both**: contract-versioned (MAJOR = a named audience's integration breaks, mechanical from a `**BREAKING:**` entry) and release-versioned (MAJOR = a committed promise lands), which is what makes `1.0` reachable rather than perpetually deferred — the answer to "when?" becomes a burn-down instead of an argument. And **breaking is always breaking *for whom***: a claim that cannot name the audience whose contract broke is not a breaking change, which is why a runtime upgrade looked breaking under the old wording and should trigger nothing
- **`docs/releases/README.md` — where a project records what its releases promise** (#225). Release identity is project data, not a standard, so it gets a home: the archetype per independently versioned thing (an application with a public API has two, and they move independently), and the named releases in sequence with their promises, audiences and status. Two workflows own the question, because **asking it only at inception is the same defect as never asking it**: most projects cannot answer before they have something to version. `/bootstrap` seeds whatever the interview settled (new spine question **`Q-SCOPE-05`**), and the **session-start release trigger** asks the first time a cut is actually due and identity is still unrecorded — the moment the answer costs nothing and is worth something. Following the founding-doc rule from #202, *"not yet decided"* is a recordable answer with a date, so a deferred call is distinguishable from an unasked one and the check can terminate
- **Release membership — one question, four answers, and the milestone as the manifest** (#226, epic #224, ADR-001). Nothing in the kit said how an item gets into a release, so the two default answers filled the gap and both fail. *"Is this valuable?"* is unbounded and two sessions rank the same backlog differently; a **label** carrying scope has to be re-judged every time the release definition moves, and nothing prompts anyone to do it. The measured consequence, from a real project: a scope label decayed into roughly **thirty freeform variants that could not be counted**, and the team **finished its release and moved on without noticing**, because an adjective on an issue has no completion event. The test that ships instead is a forced choice with a cost attached — **"would you delay the release for this?"** — and reproduces because of the cost. Four outcomes, not three: **committed** (in the milestone), **stretch** (outside it, and needing *no mechanism* — the milestone is the commitment, so everything outside it is opportunistic by definition, and a second list of "probably" items is one nobody maintains and everybody reads as a promise), **out** (dated and reasoned), and **triggered** — the category the derivation added. A developer API portal is out *until outside integration requests start*; a certification track is out *until a customer contract requires it*. Filed as "out" they look abandoned and get re-litigated; filed as **triggered** they are dormant commitments that activate on their own terms, recording **the event rather than a date**. **The milestone is the manifest**, and its load-bearing property is not tidiness but that *adding an item to it is an event*: the strongest case in the derivation was an issue whose scope changed in a beta-feedback conversation and never reached the record, still carrying wording that put it out of scope months after the audience had asked for it — the project's own "conversation is not authoritative until recorded" rule applied to release scope, where it had no owning moment. Three rules keep a manifest honest: **deferral is a recorded removal with a reason** (or a release quietly shrinks until it is trivially complete), **an item partly in and partly out is a membership bug — split it, do not judge it**, and **the pass is only as good as what it read** — a real 75-issue pass judged from titles and labels produced at least one stale label and one misleading title, both of which changed the answer
- **The `triggered` label, and the one place a label is allowed near release scope** (#226). Release scope itself is never a label — but a triggered item is the inverse: deliberately *outside* every milestone until a named event fires, with the event in its body. Without a label it is invisible, because nobody greps issue bodies, and a dormant commitment that cannot be found is indistinguishable from an abandoned one. Added to the label manifest in `ai/scripts/bootstrap-labels.sh`, with the exception and its reason stated where the rule lives

### Changed

- **Horizon, Status and milestone are now three separate scales, and the standards say so** (#226). `ROADMAP_STANDARD.md` already separated Horizon (roadmap *intent*) from Status (execution lifecycle); membership adds a third, and it is the one that commits. A `Horizon: Now` feature can sit **outside** the milestone (wanted, not blocking) and a `Later` item can be pulled **into** it the moment a published obligation turns out to require it — so reading Horizon as scope produces exactly the wrong answer in both directions. `TASK_ISSUE_STANDARD.md`'s "milestones mean releases only" gains what a milestone then *does*; `VERSIONING_AND_CHANGELOG_STANDARD.md` reconciles the tension its own trigger created — **a timeboxed cut needs no milestone; a named release does**, and ships when the milestone empties rather than when two weeks have passed
- **MAJOR leaves the versioning standard, and the dormant "After 1.0" section is retired** (#225). `VERSIONING_AND_CHANGELOG_STANDARD.md` keeps what it can actually decide — the CHANGELOG rules, the MINOR/PATCH rubric, the release trigger, the mechanics of a cut — and routes MAJOR to the release standard, because MAJOR is a statement about a promise and this standard only sees changes. The retired section is replaced rather than flipped: **no changelog section describes the event that causes a MAJOR**, so `release.sh` recommending MINOR or PATCH only is now stated as correct behaviour instead of a pre-1.0 caveat. Two rules bind regardless of archetype — a breaking entry is prefixed `**BREAKING:**` whatever the bump turns out to be, and every breaking claim names the audience whose contract broke. **Perpetual `0.x` joins the failure-modes list** as a named defect with a mitigation attached, rather than being the unexamined default state

### Fixed

- **`scripts/upgrade-smoke.sh` would have failed every future PR that ships a new standard** (#234, found while implementing #225). Its fourth assertion — *"files the upgrade delivered carry no unfilled tokens"* — filled the project's tokens **before** the upgrade and then grepped for `{{` afterwards. Every shipped standard is generic by design and arrives holding `{{PROJECT_OWNER}}` and `{{PROJECT_NAME}}`; filling it is `/bootstrap`'s retrofit job, which the test does not re-run. So the assertion effectively read *"the kit may never ship a new generic standard again"*, and it passed until now only by accident: the test landed in #194 and the last new standard shipped in #78. The regression it was reaching for is the **other** one, and that one is real, silent and worth blocking a merge over: **an upgrade must never re-tokenise a file the adopter had already adapted.** The post-upgrade verify hits are now split against a snapshot of the pre-upgrade file list — a **pre-existing** file carrying a token again fails; a **newly delivered** one is expected and named in the output as part of the adopter experience, then given the same fill pass and re-verified, so what arrived is proven *fillable* rather than merely tolerated. `bootstrap/VERIFY_IGNORE` already excludes `README.md` by name, which is why a new seeded `docs/**/README.md` slipped through while a new standard did not — an asymmetry that was itself a sign the assertion was not measuring what it meant to. Kit-dev tooling only — no shipped content changes

## [0.13.0] - 2026-08-04

### Added

- **The ADR index carries relationships, so cross-cutting effects are visible without opening every ADR** (#212). Splitting ADRs into one file each is right for their size — but the cost is that *how decisions relate* becomes invisible, and nothing paid it back: the shipped index listed title, status and filename, none of it derived from the ADR's content, so spotting that a new decision collides with, revises or depends on an existing one meant opening all of them, which nobody does. Measured against a mature adopter (54 ADRs, 5,711 lines — ~106 lines each), and the missing structure was already being routed around: that repo's index had accumulated a **~3,000-word `Updated:` field**, a running commentary on what each recent ADR revises and supersedes, because the knowledge had nowhere legitimate to live and the date stamp was the only field available. `ADR_INDEX.md` gains a **`Relates to`** column with four rules — one line per row (prose here recreates the problem), by ID and never by path, written in the same PR as the ADR, and **both ends updated** when one ADR supersedes another, since a one-way link only tells you a decision was replaced if you happen to read the replacement first. The `Updated:` stamp now says in the file that it is a date and nothing else, with what-changed belonging in the row it changed. `ADR_TEMPLATE.md`'s vague, optional **`Related ADRs:`** field becomes a typed **`Relates to:`** line (`revises` · `supersedes` · `amends` · `depends on` · `companion to`) that the index row is copied from, so the index line has a source instead of being invented at index-writing time. The index's own keep-current obligation gains an owner rather than staying implicit — `ai/CHECKLISTS/coding.md` and `/bootstrap` both now name the index row alongside the ADR, because an index nobody is routed to update is the failure #202 is about, one artifact over
- **The assessment gate — implementing a spec now includes filing its content** (#210, epic #205, T37.6). This is the step the kit exists to perform: turning interview and spec answers into the decisions and standing truth a build actually runs on. Without a gate it is also the step that **looks identical to done when skipped** — the code ships, the spec reads complete, and the rules it declared never landed anywhere durable, which is the failure pattern epic #172 was built to stop. `ai/CHECKLISTS/coding.md` gains an **Assessment** section, placed second (right after Impact Analysis) rather than filed under Documentation, because it is an implementation activity and not a paperwork afterthought: every declared row in the spec gets a destination (rules/stories/criteria/NFRs/invariants/UX clauses → `docs/registers/`, architecture → an ADR, a term → the glossary), a row deliberately **not** carried forward is recorded with its reason — *dropped silently is the failure, not the decision to drop* — and a spec cannot reach `Consumed` while it still has an unresolved Open question. **Two-way provenance is now stated as a rule** rather than left implicit in two templates: a filed row cites its spec, *and* the spec's `Landed in` names that row by ID, because neither direction alone survives a reorganisation — from the row you cannot tell what else the spec produced, and from the spec you cannot tell whether anything landed at all. The register also records why acceptance criteria carry provenance in `Traces to` rather than a second `Source` column: two columns naming one thing drift apart. **`/preflight` gains a mechanical half** (a new step 6): a diff that moves a spec to `Consumed` with an empty `Landed in` **fails**, and a spec reaching `Consumed` with no register file in the diff **warns**. The condition is greppable with essentially no false positives — if you marked it consumed, the field is either filled or it is not — so the omission is caught by machine while the checklist keeps the judgment about whether the filing was any good
- **User stories and durable acceptance criteria become first-class register artifacts** (#207, epic #205, T37.4). **User stories were absent from the entire shipped tree** — verified zero hits — so the kit shipped personas, journeys and specs while never once asking who wants a thing or why. `US-` rows land in the product register in the standard form, decomposed into `As a… / I want… / So that…` columns so the shape is learned by filling it in, with the persona named from `docs/PERSONAS.md` rather than redescribed. **`US-` replaces the `PR-` section added days earlier in #206**: written in story form, a product requirement and a user story are one statement in two grammars, and two IDs for one fact is exactly what a register exists to prevent (T37.3 amended in place; the original stands as decided). An unplanned consequence worth keeping — a "requirement" that cannot be written as a story against a real persona is almost always a mechanism, so the form performs the register-vs-ADR routing for free. **Acceptance criteria were not homeless so much as trapped**: `AC-n` already existed, but only inside the reports module's per-work-item acceptance doc, and with the criterion and its outcome in the same row — so a project without that module had no acceptance criteria at all, and every run re-authored the criteria it was about to check, which is how two runs come to disagree about one feature. The register now holds **durable** criteria (`AC-` against the story it closes, stating what "working" means for a capability), while **change-specific** criteria ("the migration backfills existing rows") stay with the work item — a routing rule the shipped tree never drew. Verification runs cite `AC-7` and record an outcome against it instead of restating it, and a durable criterion discovered mid-run is *filed to the register* rather than written into the run's notes. **No second tracing scheme** (the risk #163/T17.8 flagged): an `AC-` row cites exactly what it always cited — journey step numbers, `EC-n`, `INV-n`. Consumers rewired to match: `/qa` and the QA checklist read the register first and work with or without the reports module, `UAT_SOURCE_OF_TRUTH.md` puts the register above the UAT doc (which no longer holds durable criteria) and above feature specs (authoritative about what was *proposed*, never about what is true), the acceptance-doc template cites rather than restates and gains a register-coverage block, and diagnostic bundles name the failing criterion by ID
- **`docs/registers/PRODUCT_REGISTER.md` — a home for application-scope standing truth** (#206, epic #205, T37.2/T37.3). Business rules and product requirements that hold across the whole application previously had **no home at all**: they were buried in whichever feature spec first mentioned them, or went unwritten — the silent half of T37, and worse than the catchall the topic was opened about. The register ships typed sections with permanent ID prefixes — `BR-` business rules, `US-` user stories with their `AC-` acceptance criteria, `NFR-` quality targets, `INV-` invariants (each naming the evidence that proves it), `UX-` product-wide wording clauses — and replaces the shipped decision log's *"when uncertain where a decision belongs, check existing patterns in both locations"* with a test that resolves on its own: **would this still be true if the product were rebuilt on a different stack?** Yes → register; no → ADR. Guess-from-precedent is how a register decays into a catchall, so it is explicitly not the rule. An outbound table routes what does *not* belong — vocabulary to the glossary, working conventions to `ai/STANDARDS/`, external obligations to the compliance register, scope to the tracker — the gap that let a mature adopter record nine architecture decisions in **both** an ADR and the decision log. Two conventions make the file safe to reorganise later: **IDs are permanent addresses** (never renumbered, never reused), and **references cite the ID, never the path**. Rationale never enters a register row — the row holds the statement plus a `Source:` pointer to the spec, ADR or interview answer that decided it, so entries stay one line and the file stays readable end to end, which is the point: a contradiction between two rules is only visible when both are on screen. Sized deliberately against real data — a mature adopter's 54 ADRs average ~106 lines each, so an entire rule register costs less to load than two ADRs, and it therefore stays **one file** with splitting permitted but never scheduled. `/bootstrap` seeds it from the interview answers rather than installing it empty, and per-row `Decided` dates carry currency instead of a single file stamp that would be stale the day after it was written

### Changed

- **The routing rule decomposes before it routes, and asks its two questions in the order they resolve** (#222, found by the T37 replay #215). Measured against 90 real decision-log entries, the shipped rule decided on its own **23%** of the time — but almost none of the remainder were "the rule gave no answer". They were entries making **more than one statement**: a domain rule *and* a schema change, a vocabulary *and* a permission. **59% of entries produced rows in two or more homes**, 7% in three. The register assumed you arrive holding one statement; real decisions arrive as compound documents, and the step doing the actual work — split first, then route each part — was unwritten. It is now the rule's first move, and one decision producing several records in several homes is stated as the normal case rather than an awkward edge. Second defect, same paragraph: **the rebuild test cannot decide whether something is register material at all.** Ten percent of entries pass it cleanly and belong nowhere near a register — a scope call, a term, a working convention, a licensing obligation. The outbound table now runs **first** ("is this a register row?"), the rebuild test **second** ("register or ADR?"). Re-running the corpus under the revised rule lifted per-statement resolution from 23% to **96%**, with no destination reclassified. Three further fixes came from that re-run, and the residuals were more informative than the headline: four of six were rules the register **already had, buried inside section bodies** — invisible at the moment you are choosing a section — so the tie-breaks are now signposted where the choice is made. The two genuine gaps are closed: **a number does not make something an `NFR-`** (an enforced quota like "100 GB per organisation" is a business rule; an NFR is how *well* the product performs), and **seed, demo and fixture content gets a home** (an ADR — it is a property of the build, and the product's rules do not change because a demo org exists)
- **BREAKING:** **Independent reviewer agents now judge against the registers, never a feature spec** (#211, epic #205, T37.8). The reviewers landed in #163 a few hours before T37 was decided, taking feature-specific invariants (`INV-n`) and UX clauses (`UX-n`) from the spec. Under T37 a spec is a **proposal** — possibly superseded — so a reviewer could judge a running application against intent that was proposed and never implemented, or that was later revised, and **report a confident, evidence-backed failure against a requirement nobody holds any more**. Nothing in the review design detects that, because the evidence genuinely does show a mismatch; only the source was wrong. This makes the reviewers strictly more correct rather than merely differently wired. The fix extends the module's existing independence doctrine instead of sitting beside it: the rule was already *"a reviewer may read what should be **true**, never how something was **done**"*, and that now sharpens to a **source** — the product register plus the project's standards, both of which are kept current. The may-read/must-not-read table gains the distinction in its own row: a feature spec may be read for **orientation** (journey narrative, edge cases, the data-touchpoints map) and never as **the basis of a verdict**, and where a spec and a register row disagree the register wins — the disagreement itself meaning the spec was never fully consumed. Rewired: `review-uat-driver` takes `INV-n` from the register, `review-ux-conformance` cites register `UX-n` clauses, `/review` builds its brief from the journey registry plus the register rather than the spec, and `INDEPENDENT_REVIEW_STANDARD.md` carries the rationale once with the agents pointing back at it (T11). `review-data-verifier` keeps reading the spec's data-touchpoints map — that is navigation, not judgment — but now states the boundary explicitly: **a spec is orientation, never authority**, and where storage disagrees with the map, storage is reported and the stale map is itself a finding. `docs/specs/README.md` no longer claims the reviewers consume the spec
- **BREAKING:** **A feature spec is now a proposal, and is deliberately no longer kept current** (#208, epic #205, T37.1/T37.5, revising **T17.8(a)**). The released rule said behavior changes and their spec land in the same PR — sound when a spec was the only durable description of a feature, wrong once registers exist. A spec is authoritative about what was **proposed**, never about what the product currently does, so editing a consumed spec to match later behavior destroys the record of what was agreed and yields a second, weaker copy of the register. `DOCUMENTATION_STANDARD.md`'s keep-current rule now names **register rows** and states the spec exclusion positively, so a future editor doesn't read the absence as an oversight. The deeper reframe: **rot was the symptom of one document being asked to be both the proposal and the record of the system** — splitting them removes the failure rather than policing it, and a document that never claimed to be current cannot go stale. T17.8(d)'s planned spec-vs-behavior drift lens falls away for the same reason (divergence is the expected state after implementation, not drift); (b) UAT traceability and (c) interview stress-testing stand unchanged. **Status gains `Consumed`** in place of `Implemented`, plus a **`Landed in`** field naming the permanent homes the content became (`BR-014`, `AC-007`, `ADR-012`) — so consumption is a gate rather than a habit, and a spec reaching `Consumed` with an empty `Landed in` is the failure this design has to catch (the mechanical half is #210). Why this matters beyond bookkeeping: **a proposal carrying no authority until consumed is what makes a spec safe to accept from anyone** — a collaborator, or a collaborator's AI working from these templates — because nothing in it binds the product until someone with the architectural context assesses and files it. `docs/specs/README.md` becomes the doctrine's single home, and specs leave the source-of-truth precedence as inputs; the glossary entry and the kit-docs routing table now separate what a feature *currently does* (the register) from what it was *proposed* to do (its spec). T17.8 amended in place with a dated stamp — the original stands as decided

### Removed

- **BREAKING:** **`docs/decision-log.md` no longer ships, and the source-of-truth precedence order changes** (#209, epic #205, T37.5). The log was the catchall T37 was opened about, and its own shipped text said so — *"when uncertain where a decision belongs, check existing patterns in both locations."* It is deleted rather than narrowed, because **a decision is an *event*, not a content type**: a category defined by how something happened rather than what it says is the exact shape that becomes a catchall, which is why narrowing it would only have reset the clock. What a decision *produces* already has a home — a mechanism to an ADR, a rule/story/criterion/NFR/invariant/UX clause to `docs/registers/`, a term to the glossary, a whether/when call to the tracker, an external obligation to the compliance register — and the **reasoning** stays in the `docs/plans/` working doc, cited by the record's `Source:` field, so the statement is one line and the argument one hop away. Validated against 90 real ShelterSync decisions: every kind lands, and the nine entries that duplicated an ADR simply disappear. The cost is named rather than hidden: **the single chronological narrative goes away** — per-row `Decided` dates make chronology reconstructable and `docs/plans/` keeps each argument, but the end-to-end read of how a product was reasoned into existence is distributed. New precedence: schema → **ADRs** → **registers** → journey registry → UAT docs → tracked issues, with **feature specs deliberately absent** (a proposal, never authoritative about what the product does). The sweep ran to **34 references across 20 files**, not the 12 the issue estimated — including six that had nothing to do with decision homes and would have gone stale silently: the interview standard's provenance machinery (`Derived:`/`Source:` examples, the supersede pattern, the rule of altitude), the task-issue standard's governing-decisions table, the coding checklist's completion gate, the changelog standard's doc-only exemption, the question bank's downstream note, and the file-naming rule that used `decision-log.md` as its worked example of a working doc. **Existing adopters keep theirs**: the scaffold has no delete path and never removes a file, so a repo that upgrades keeps its log untouched — migration is a `/conform` concern, and `/conform` gains the step that routes an existing log entry by entry, checks first whether an entry merely restates an ADR that already exists, and **never deletes the log**, leaving it stamped historical until a human confirms every entry has landed

### Fixed

- **The retrofit path now routes founding docs it installed empty, and refuses to install a second decision home** (#202, found by the kit's own retrofit in #184). Two gaps, both silent. (a) `/bootstrap`'s retrofit *mentioned* "founding docs that never got seeded" and nothing afterwards said which ones were empty or asked whether they should be filled — the kit's own retrofit left five empty and nobody noticed until a human opened one by chance. There is now a **founding-doc audit**: after anything installs, check each founding doc for real content versus the shipped skeleton (placeholder rows, `YYYY-MM-DD` stamps, `WORKED EXAMPLE` blocks), report the empty ones as one short list, and offer to seed each from what the repo already knows — code, README, existing docs, the tracker — the way an inception brief is drafted. `docs/registers/PRODUCT_REGISTER.md` is **reported first**: it sits at #3 in the source-of-truth precedence, so an empty one puts the top of the hierarchy on a file with nothing in it. The audit matters most on **upgrades**, which deliver new founding docs into a repo that never ran inception for them. (b) The retrofit installed a decision skeleton without checking whether the repo already had one — the kit had recorded 36 decisions in `docs/plans/` and got `docs/architecture/decisions/` and a decision log installed beside them. A **collision check** now runs before any decision or register skeleton: look for a `decisions/`, `adr/`, `rfc/` or `architecture/` directory, a decision log under any name, a requirements document with IDs, or a wiki equivalent; a same-role home is one holding landed architectural decisions or a typed decision/requirement register (**T37.9**, the definition this issue was waiting on); found one → stop and ask, never install beside it. Two homes for one job with no rule saying which wins is worse than either alone *and* silent — the repo looks more organised, not less. Migration hands off to `/conform`, which already routes entries one at a time and never deletes the original. The load-bearing addition beyond what was filed: **"empty on purpose" is now a recordable answer**. An empty skeleton is indistinguishable from a register someone considered and left empty, so a doc deliberately left blank takes a one-line note saying so — without it the audit cannot terminate, re-reports the same docs every run, and decays back into the mention it replaced. The product register states the convention, borrowing it from the compliance register's long-standing rule that out-of-scope is a valid status but has to be recorded rather than left blank. `DOCUMENTATION_STANDARD` § "Setup blocks need a workflow owner" generalizes to cover the whole class: a PR shipping a new founding doc wires the workflow that reports it empty, in the same PR
- `scripts/self-conform.py` stripped only one of the **two** genericization-banner forms (#200) — the italic line used by standards and commands, but not the HTML-comment form used by templates and seeded docs. Three derived files (`ai/TEMPLATES/GITHUB_ISSUE_TEMPLATE.md`, `ai/TEMPLATES/TASK_ISSUE_TEMPLATE.md`, `docs/architecture/decisions/ADR_TEMPLATE.md`) therefore kept a banner marking them un-adapted, which `/evergreen`'s standards-drift lens is meant to flag. Kit-dev tooling only — no shipped content changes
- **The bootstrap verify step could report a clean repo that still had unfilled tokens** (#192, #190) — three defects in one snippet, all found only when the kit ran its own adoption path. (a) The pattern was `\{\{[A-Z_]+\}\}`, which **excludes digits**, so `{{E2E_COMMAND}}` — a core token in `bootstrap/PLACEHOLDERS.md` — was invisible to it; a project could fill everything, see verify pass, and ship `{{E2E_COMMAND}}` unfilled in its testing standard, QA checklist and `/qa` command. (b) The `--include` list covered `*.md *.sh *.json *.txt` only, so `.github/workflows/pr-validation.yml.example` — which the kit ships carrying **five** tokens — was never scanned at all. (c) GitHub Actions expression syntax (`${{ matrix.os }}`, `${{ secrets.X }}`) matches the verify's `{{` and produced false hits for any adopter whose CI uses it. Fixed in both places the snippet lives (`/bootstrap` § Verify and `bootstrap/SETUP.md` § Verify, which must not drift apart): the pattern now allows digits, the include list gains `*.yml`/`*.yaml`/`*.example`, and `bootstrap/VERIFY_IGNORE` ships the Actions-syntax exclusion by default rather than leaving every adopter to rediscover it

## [0.12.0] - 2026-08-03

### Added
- **Every shipped standard now carries a `Last Updated: YYYY-MM-DD` line**, and `DOCUMENTATION_STANDARD.md` gains the rule that requires one (#173, epic #172). Previously 2 of 19 standards had the field and **no rule anywhere asked for it** — it was never a convention that decayed, just two files that happened to have it, and both of their dates were wrong (`GIT_WORKFLOW` claimed 2026-07-09 against a 2026-07-13 change; `VERSIONING` claimed 2026-06-24 against the same). Each of the 19 is stamped with its **actual** last content-change date, not today's. The rule states the trigger precisely — a PR changing a standard's *content* bumps the date; typo, formatting, and metadata-only edits do not — and records why the field is not redundant with version control: **standards get distributed**, so the reader is usually holding a copy in a repo whose history says nothing about where the text came from, and an absent or stale date reads as "current" to someone with no way to check. `/evergreen`'s date-sweep lens now points at that rule instead of describing the field it could not previously assume existed
